\section{Statedness Diagnostic}
\label{app:benchmarks}

This section gives the episode-generation procedures, dataset splits, candidate pools and controls for the statedness diagnostic in \S\ref{sec:design}.


\subsection{Passages, candidates and future questions}

GPT-4.1 generates passages about named people, candidate concepts with gold utility labels, and future questions and answers. Explicit episodes state the useful concept. Implied-answer training episodes imply a useful concept that supplies the answer, including episodes from the alternate-generator set. Decoupled episodes require an implied useful concept to support a further inference: the answer is absent from the passage and candidates, and the question shares no passage content word except the person's name. Compositional episodes require a multi-hop inference and permit question anchors from the passage. The Decoupled and Compositional development sets guide configuration selection and are excluded from gradient training.

Rule-based filters check candidate strings, useful-concept absence where required, answer exclusion and the Decoupled question's lexical overlap. Deduplication removes repeated concepts across retained episodes. Leakage filters check full lexemes, inflected answers and question anchors. A blind GPT-6-Astra alias filter receives the future question, gold answer and useful concept. It rejects episodes whose useful concept equals, contains or is a variant, synonym, translation or abbreviation of the answer. For example, \emph{caterer/catering} is an alias, whereas \emph{Madrid/Spanish} requires a further inference.


\subsection{Episode-generation prompt}
\label{app:authorprompt}
GPT-4.1 receives the system instruction, ``You are a careful dataset designer for a cognitive-science experiment on LLM memory.'' Prompt~\ref{prompt:author} transcribes the generation constraints; the output-format instruction requests one structured record per episode. Generation uses temperature 1.0 and batches of up to five episodes.


\subsection{Held-out and replication sets}
\label{app:fresh}

The held-out and test sets use the Decoupled construction prompt, followed by rule-based filtering, deduplication and disjointness filtering. A passage matching a prior set after lowercasing and trimming is excluded. Episodes are also excluded when their useful concept matches a labeled useful concept in the training episodes. The test set excludes passages appearing in the held-out set. Shared answer categories, such as languages and currencies, are permitted. The alternate-generator set uses GPT-4o for the Implied-answer construction (App.~\ref{app:replications}).

Development uses Decoupled (68 episodes), Compositional (52), and two separate 68-episode sets that add confusable or unrelated unstated distractors to Decoupled.

Statedness uses the case-insensitive stem match specified in \S\ref{sec:statedness}. Table~\ref{tab:pools} lists the pools and controls, their construction, candidate counts and comparisons.

\paragraph{Construction rules.}
To name the useful concept in the original pool, we append ``The passage also mentions \emph{concept}.'' with the useful concept substituted.

For the pool with unstated distractors, an LLM generates four lowercase single-word distractors from the useful concept's semantic category. They must be confusable alternatives that the passage neither states nor implies. The response must be comma-separated. Candidates matching an original candidate or occurring as a case-insensitive substring of the passage are discarded; accepted distractors are appended in response order. This adds 1,450 candidates: four in 361 episodes and three in episodes 24 and 263, for 3,057 candidates. The Decoupled development set with confusable unstated distractors uses the same procedure.

For the statedness-balanced pool, odd positions, counting from one, retain the original passage; even positions append the sentence naming the useful concept. Every episode retains its original candidates and appends the first four accepted additions at odd positions or the first three at even positions from the pool with unstated distractors, preserving order. This gives 2,877 candidates.

The naming-balanced pool uses the same ordered candidate lists and starts from the original passages. It names four candidates per episode: four distractors at odd positions, or the useful concept and three distractors at even positions. One random generator with seed 0 samples uniformly without replacement in fixed episode order. Selected candidates are shuffled before naming sentences are constructed. The five sentence forms rotate: ``The passage also mentions \emph{concept}.'', ``\emph{concept} is relevant here.'', ``Note that \emph{concept} comes up as well.'', ``There is also \emph{concept} to consider.'', and ``\emph{concept} features in this as well.'' The first selected candidate uses the form at the episode's position in this cycle; subsequent candidates advance one form. Each sentence is inserted at a boundary set by episode and insertion index, counting earlier insertions. There are 1,452 naming sentences, 181 naming the useful concept.

The enlarged pool concatenates the original candidates, accepted same-category distractors, and four useful concepts from subsequent episodes, in that order. Selection starts with the next episode and advances cyclically. A useful concept is accepted as a distractor only when it is absent from the passage and original candidate list and differs from previously accepted cross-episode distractors. Selection stops after four accepted concepts. Concatenation retains the first occurrence of each concept, giving 4,506 candidates. The Decoupled development set with unrelated unstated distractors uses only the original candidates and these four cross-episode additions.

In the original pool, useful concepts are stated in 5\% of test-set episodes, and 99\% of distractors are stated. In 95\% of episodes, the useful concept is the sole unstated candidate.

\paragraph{Passage and useful-concept replacement.}
Passage replacement uses a fixed episode permutation to replace each passage while preserving its candidates, utility labels, question and answer. Useful-concept replacement applies when SDA's top-two selections contain the useful concept. It keeps the other selection and replaces the useful concept with the next-ranked candidate. The same frozen reader then answers the same question again. This gives 249 replacement episodes (App.~\ref{app:episodemetrics}).

\paragraph{Paired-passage set.}
\label{app:pairedpassage}
The paired-passage set uses the 363 test-set episodes. Matching them into 181 disjoint pairs gives 362 episodes, with each source episode in at most one pair and one episode unpaired. Each member retains its passage, future question and answer. Its own useful concept is the only candidate with a positive gold label; its partner's useful concept is a distractor. Each candidate list is the union of the two original lists, averaging 8.86 candidates per episode, with between 8 and 12 candidates. Pair members contain the same candidates in different shuffled orders; neither retains its original order. In all 362 episodes, both useful concepts are unstated in both passages. Statedness therefore cannot distinguish the useful concept from its partner's useful concept; the passage determines which is useful. SDA uses the development-selected configuration. \section{Self-Distilled Admission}
\label{app:training}

This section gives the algorithm, teacher construction, data split, training configuration, timing procedures and capability measurements for Self-Distilled Admission in \S\ref{sec:sda}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Self-Distilled Admission.}
\label{alg:sda}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frozen model $\mathcal M$; training episodes $\mathcal E$, each a passage $x$ with candidates $C$
\For{each episode $(x,C)\in\mathcal E$}
  \For{each candidate $c\in C$}
    \State $R(c;x)\gets$ peak reciprocal rank across layers \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:wrr}}
    \State $\mathrm{LL}(c;x)\gets\log p_{\mathcal M}(c\mid x)/|c|$
  \EndFor
  \State $T(c;x)\gets\tfrac12\bigl(P_R(c;x)+P_{\mathrm{LL}}(c;x)\bigr)$ for all $c\in C$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:teacher}}
  \State $\tilde y(c;x)\gets 1$ for the two highest $T$, else $0$
\EndFor
\State train adapter $\theta$ on $\mathcal L(\theta)$ over all $(x,c,\tilde y)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}}
\State select the earliest checkpoint with the highest validation within-episode AUC
\Statex \textbf{Deployment:} given a passage $x$, candidates $C$ and budget $k$
\State store the $k$ candidates with the highest $s_\theta(c;x)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Teacher construction and data split}

Within each episode, $r_S(c)$ is the ascending mid-rank of candidate $c$ under score $S$; ties receive the mean of their occupied ranks. For $m>1$ candidates, the percentile is
\begin{equation}
P_S(c;x)=\frac{r_S(c)-1}{m-1},
\qquad S\in\{R,\mathrm{LL}\}.
\label{eq:percentile}
\end{equation}
A singleton pool receives percentile one half. The mean of the percentiles gives the teacher in Eq.~\eqref{eq:teacher}. Descending teacher score determines the top-2 labels, with candidate order breaking ties.

Training and validation use three generator-and-construction groups: Explicit (88 episodes), implied-answer from the primary generator (75), and implied-answer from the alternate generator (57). The split is stratified by group and keeps each episode's candidates together. Within each group, episodes are sorted by a hash of the source-qualified episode identity under a fixed seed. The lowest 20\%, rounded up, enter validation. The groups contain 70 training and 18 validation episodes, 60 training and 15 validation episodes, and 45 training and 12 validation episodes, respectively: 175 training and 45 validation episodes in total. Checkpoint selection follows \S\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Scoring time and throughput}
\label{app:timing}

We time Qwen3-8B on one A100-80GB using an importance prompt for each of the original pool's 1,607 candidates. vLLM enables prefix caching and LoRA serving. After warming the engine on an initial subset, we time batched generation over the full candidate list. Each prompt generates one token greedily. SDA attaches the adapter to the admission request; the importance report uses the same engine without the adapter. Model loading is excluded. Milliseconds per candidate equal wall time divided by candidate count; throughput is candidate count divided by wall time.

Offline selections use one reference run of the three SDA training runs (\S\ref{sec:setup}). Served and offline selections go to the same frozen reader and semantic judge. The serving engine recovers yes/no probability masses from returned token log probabilities.

\paragraph{Deployment.}\label{sec:deployment}
With prefix caching, vLLM running Qwen3-8B on one A100-80GB serves SDA at 242 candidates per second (4.1\,ms each), versus the importance report's 290 (3.4\,ms), over the original pool's 1,607 candidates. Served answer accuracy at $k=2$ is 50.1\%, within 0.3 points of offline, with 91.7\% agreement in the top two stored concepts.



\subsection{Capability with the adapter enabled on every token}
\label{app:capability}

With the adapter enabled on every token, the four measured capability scores change by at most 0.3 percentage points under matched prompts. Base and adapter scores are 71.6\% and 71.5\% on MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, 80.4\% and 80.7\% on ARC-Easy, 56.2\% and 56.4\% on ARC-Challenge \citep{clark2018arc}, and 88.4\% and 88.6\% on the GSM8K evaluation subset \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}.


\section{Experimental Settings}
\label{app:repro}

This section gives the admission baselines, published admission criteria, prompt and scoring procedures, and semantic grading protocol for the experimental settings in \S\ref{sec:settings}.


\subsection{Admission baselines}
\label{app:baselines}

The admission baselines are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Importance report}: the unadapted model's score $V$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}.
\item \textbf{1--10 importance rating}: the expected rating under normalized probabilities of complete answer strings.
\item \textbf{Reversed importance report}: ranks the lowest importance report scores first.
\item \textbf{Unstated-first}: prioritizes unstated candidates and breaks ties at random.
\item \textbf{Unstated-first with report ordering}: prioritizes unstated candidates and preserves importance report ordering within each statedness group.
\item \textbf{Combined teacher used directly}: ranks directly by Eq.~\eqref{eq:teacher}.
\item \textbf{Implied-content wording}: asks the importance judgment to consider what the passage implies. \textbf{Statedness-cued wording} asks whether storing the candidate helps even if the passage never mentions it (App.~\ref{app:wording}).
\item \textbf{Output likelihood} and \textbf{logit-lens readout}: rank directly by their respective scores.
\item \textbf{Gold-concept selection}: a reference policy that stores the useful concept first and fills the remaining slots in candidate order.
\end{itemize}
The closed-book and full-context readers provide answer accuracy references.


\subsection{Published admission criteria}
\label{app:publishedbaselines}

\paragraph{ConsistencyGate.}
ConsistencyGate \citep{consistencygate2026} scores how strongly the passage supports a candidate. We implement two forms. The sampled form follows their Equations~2 and~3 and uses their Appendix~C prompt verbatim, with five samples at temperature one and at most eight new tokens per sample. Its score is the mean of the parsed decimals. The single-pass form follows their Equation~4 variant, posing the same question in yes/no form and scoring the probability of yes under a softmax over yes and no token log-probabilities. On the original pool, its 0.7 threshold admits 26.7\% of useful concepts and 70.8\% of all candidates. Within-episode AUC is 0.133 for single-pass scoring and 0.190 for sampled scoring.

\paragraph{A-MAC.}
A-MAC \citep{amac2026} combines five factors. Utility uses one temperature-zero call asking whether the candidate is actionable, supports likely follow-up questions, or captures a persistent constraint or preference. Confidence is the maximum ROUGE-L between the candidate and a supporting passage sentence. The type prior uses their rule-based entity cue. Novelty compares against stored memory, and recency depends on elapsed time. Each episode starts with empty memory and gives all candidates the same timestamp, so novelty and recency do not affect ordering and are omitted. The three remaining factors form a weighted sum with utility weight one. We search confidence and type-prior weights from 0.0 to 2.0 in steps of 0.1, select them by within-episode AUC in five-fold cross-validation over the 363 episodes of the statedness-balanced pool, and evaluate the fitted combination out of fold. Every fold selects zero weight for confidence and type prior. The fitted combination and utility alone both have out-of-fold within-episode AUC 0.6795.

\paragraph{Prediction error.}
Nemori's prediction-error criterion \citep{nemori2026} scores what the model fails to predict. We score each candidate by its surprisal under the passage, the negative of output likelihood, and rank candidates for admission. This implementation uses the candidate and passage without stored memory.

All three published admission criteria receive the candidate and passage with the future question hidden and store the top-$k$ candidates under the shared budget. Prompts~\ref{prompt:cgsample} to~\ref{prompt:amac} give the three scoring instructions.


\subsection{Importance prompt, frozen reader and rating scoring}
\label{app:prompts}

Appendix~\ref{app:promptlist} lists the prompts. Each is a user message rendered with the model's chat template and assistant generation prefix. SDA and the importance report use the same importance prompt (Prompt~\ref{prompt:admission}).

The affirmative token set contains the distinct first tokens of \emph{yes} in lowercase, initial-capital and uppercase forms, with and without a leading space. The negative set uses the corresponding forms of \emph{no}. Both policies use Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}; SDA enables its adapter for admission.

The frozen reader (Prompt~\ref{prompt:reader}) answers with the admission adapter disabled. The closed-book reference supplies ``Answer this question concisely.'' followed by the question and answer prefix. The full-context reference supplies the passage, then ``Answer concisely.'' followed by the same question and answer prefix. The frozen reader uses greedy decoding.

Let $a_r$ be the complete answer string for rating $r$, and $u_R(c,x)$ the rating prompt (Prompt~\ref{prompt:rating}). Each answer string is scored by multiplying the conditional probabilities of all its tokens. The expected rating is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\bar r(c;x)&=\sum_{r=1}^{10}r\,\pi_r(c;x),\\
\pi_r(c;x)&=\frac{p_{\mathcal M}(a_r\mid u_R(c,x))}
{\sum_{j=1}^{10}p_{\mathcal M}(a_j\mid u_R(c,x))}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:rating}
\end{equation}



\subsection{Blind semantic judge}
\label{app:judge}

GPT-6-Astra grades the response against the question and gold answer without the admission-policy identity. Prompt~\ref{prompt:grading} gives the grading instruction verbatim; the output-format instruction requests a label for each supplied answer. Correct responses count toward answer accuracy; hedged and incorrect responses count as errors. Identical question, gold answer and response triples reuse the same judgment. Normalized string matching provides a second grade for each answer. The graders agree on 85.1\% of 13,068 answers. SDA exceeds the importance report in every training run under both graders.

\section{TwoHopFact Replication}\label{app:twohop}
This section gives the TwoHopFact construction, reader-only filters, four-condition win rates and results by stated and implied episodes for \S\ref{sec:twohop}.

We construct episodes from TwoHopFact \citep{yang2024latent} two-hop facts, $e_1 \rightarrow e_2 \rightarrow e_3$, with the bridge entity $e_2$ as the useful concept. Each base passage contains three templated sentences: a scene sentence, a middle sentence describing $e_2$ through $e_1$ without naming $e_2$ (e.g., ``the main character of the movie Alien''), and a closing sentence. The future question asks for $e_3$ through the protagonist, e.g., ``Which actor played the character Liesel kept reading about?'' Stated episodes append ``The passage also mentions \texttt{<e2>}.''

Candidates follow the main statedness-balanced pool's composition: the useful concept; three stated distractors, comprising the scene sentence's time and place nouns and the closing sentence's noun; and three same-category unstated distractors in stated episodes or four in implied episodes. The head entity $e_1$ is not a candidate because the reader's first-hop knowledge turns it into a second route to the answer. The retained set averages 7.55 candidates per episode. Useful concepts constitute 13.2\% of stated candidates and 13.3\% of unstated candidates; ranking candidates by statedness alone yields a within-episode AUC of 0.497.

We apply three reader-only filters sequentially using the frozen Qwen3-8B reader. Of 7,005 episodes, 840 pass the first-hop knowledge check. Of these, 216 yield a correct answer to the future question with the useful concept alone stored. Retaining those on which the reader fails closed-book leaves 211 episodes: 96 stated and 115 implied. We evaluate budgets $k=1,2,3$ with the same blind semantic judge as in the main experiments. SDA is the Qwen3-8B adapter trained on the generated episodes, applied without training on TwoHopFact.

Table~\ref{tab:twohopgrid} reports four-condition win rates. Conditions A and B compare the implied useful concept with stated and unstated distractors, respectively; conditions C and D compare the stated useful concept with stated and unstated distractors, respectively. Unstated-first with report ordering ranks every unstated candidate ahead of every stated candidate and uses the importance report within each group, so its A of 100.0\% and D of 0.0\% follow from the rule.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:promptlist}

The prompts below give the instructions for the evaluated models, the
generator and the judge. Prompt~\ref{prompt:author} is the prose
transcription described in App.~\ref{app:authorprompt}.
Italic phrases stand for the supplied passage, candidate, stored concepts or
question.

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{Importance prompt, shared by SDA and the report}[prompt:admission]
\emph{Passage}

Based only on the passage above, is the concept ``\emph{candidate
concept}'' one of the most important things to remember in order to
answer possible future questions? Answer with a single word: yes or no.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{1--10 importance rating}[prompt:rating]
\emph{Passage}

On a scale of 1 to 10, where 1 is purely mundane and 10 is extremely
important, rate how important it is to remember the concept
``\emph{candidate concept}'' in order to answer possible future questions
about this passage. Answer with a single number.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{ConsistencyGate, sampled form}[prompt:cgsample]
You are a fact-checking assistant. Given a source context and a
candidate fact, rate how strongly the fact is supported by the context.

Source context: \emph{passage}\\
Candidate fact: \emph{candidate concept}\\
Question: How strongly is this candidate fact supported by the source
context above? Do not use outside knowledge.

0.0 = completely unsupported or directly contradicted by the context\\
0.5 = partially supported or ambiguous\\
1.0 = fully and directly supported by the context

Output only a single decimal number between 0.0 and 1.0.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{ConsistencyGate, single-pass form}[prompt:cgyesno]
You are a fact-checking assistant. Given a source context and a
candidate fact, decide whether the fact is supported by the context.

Source context: \emph{passage}\\
Candidate fact: \emph{candidate concept}\\
Question: Is this candidate fact supported by the source context above?
Do not use outside knowledge.

Answer with a single word: yes or no.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{A-MAC utility}[prompt:amac]
\emph{passage}

Consider storing the concept ``\emph{candidate concept}'' in long-term
memory. Is it actionable, does it support likely follow-up questions, or
does it capture a persistent constraint or preference?

Answer with a single word: yes or no.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{Frozen reader}[prompt:reader]
Earlier you read a passage. The only things you remember from it are:
\emph{stored concepts separated by commas}.\\
Using only what you remember, answer concisely.\\
Question: \emph{future question}\\
Answer:
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{Episode generation}[prompt:author]
Design a batch of diverse test episodes about silent bridge reasoning
for a memory experiment. Each episode has a passage of four to eight
sentences about a named person; invent a distinctive first name,
different in every episode, and vary the domain widely. A question
asked later, out of context, refers to the episode only through the
person's name and requires an unstated bridge fact inferable from the
passage, followed by common general knowledge. Supply a short correct
answer of one to three words and five to seven candidate concepts,
each a common lowercase word with a utility category and a short reason.

Exactly one candidate is the useful bridge concept, and its word must
be absent from the passage. The question must share no passage content
word except the person's name: someone who never read the passage
must be unable to answer it. For a passage about Elena wandering the
Plaza Mayor, ask what language Elena should have brushed up on for her
trip, without naming the plaza in the question. The answer must differ
from every candidate and be absent from the passage; combining the
bridge with general knowledge yields the answer. For example, the
Plaza Mayor cues \emph{madrid}, which supports \emph{spanish}. A reader
remembering only the bridge plus the question must be able to answer.

Include two or three vivid, emotional or surprising distractors that
occur literally in the passage but are useless for the question, and
one or two neutral concepts that occur in the passage. Every candidate
must be a single common English lowercase word. Do not reuse concepts
or person names across episodes.
\end{sdabox}

\begin{sdabox}[prompt]{Blind semantic judge}[prompt:grading]
Grade short answers to memory questions. For each item decide whether
ANSWER gives GOLD as its answer.\\
Labels:\\
``correct'': the answer commits to GOLD or an exact equivalent (synonym,
inflection, fuller name) as its single answer. Extra explanation is fine.\\
``hedged'': it offers two or more alternative answers (e.g.\ ``X or Y''),
or states the answer cannot be determined, even if GOLD is mentioned.\\
``incorrect'': anything else, including a different answer.
\end{sdabox}
