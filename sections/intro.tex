External memory systems such as MemGPT, Mem0, and A-MEM let LLM agents retain information across interactions \citep{packer2023memgpt,mem0,amem2025}. Agents must choose what to store under a budget before future questions arrive. To make this choice, memory systems ask the model how important a candidate is, using importance judgments such as Generative Agents' 1--10 importance rating \citep{park2023generative} and published admission criteria such as ConsistencyGate and A-MAC \citep{consistencygate2026,amac2026}. Does the importance judgment track what a passage means, or only what it states? A future question that combines a passage with world knowledge depends on what the passage implies.

Figure~\ref{fig:episode} shows how this distinction changes an agent's memory. Yara examines cuneiform tablets at a dig on a river that fed early agriculture, but the passage never names Mesopotamia. A later question asks which modern country she visited. The model's yes/no importance report stores \texttt{silt} and \texttt{tablet}; the frozen reader answers from the stored concepts and its own knowledge but cannot name a country. In contrast, storing \texttt{mesopotamia} with \texttt{tablet} lets the frozen reader answer Iraq. Here, recovering \texttt{mesopotamia} requires a bridging inference \citep{haviland1974whatsnew}, which links the passage to prior knowledge through a concept the passage leaves unstated. When the passage instead names the useful concept, the same useful concept becomes a passage word.

This distinction is grounded in research on text comprehension. Readers remember the situation a text describes, including inferences, more than its wording \citep{bransford1972sentence,kintsch1988construction}. For memory admission, this requires separating two properties of a candidate: statedness, whether the passage states it, and usefulness, whether a future question needs it. In Figure~\ref{fig:episode}, \texttt{silt} is stated but not useful, and \texttt{mesopotamia} is useful but unstated. Memory built for future questions must therefore admit candidates by usefulness, whatever their statedness.

We hypothesize that importance judgments instead exhibit a \textbf{Statedness Bias}: a tendency to judge a concept important because the passage states it. The yes/no importance prompt asks for support in the passage, and passage words carry the most direct support; published admission criteria score that support explicitly \citep{consistencygate2026,amac2026}. This preference therefore builds a memory that keeps the passage's words and loses what it implies.

Memory research, however, emphasizes end-to-end performance on conversational benchmarks such as LoCoMo and LongMemEval \citep{maharana2024locomo,wu2025longmemeval}. These benchmarks score whether a system retrieves and uses what a conversation contains, without separating stated from implied information. Whether LLM admission policies store what a passage implies has therefore remained untested.

We test this hypothesis with admission episodes, each a passage with its candidates and a future question, that separate statedness from usefulness. We define win rate as the episode-averaged share of comparisons in which a policy ranks the useful concept above a distractor, counting ties half. To isolate the effect of statedness, we cross the statedness of the useful concept with that of each distractor (\S\ref{sec:design}): comparing an implied useful concept against stated distractors exposes the bias, and comparing it against unstated distractors shows what the model knows. We measure win rates and answer accuracy on a statedness-balanced pool, in which ranking by statedness alone scores at chance. As a second pool, we use the original candidate pool, in which the useful concept is usually the only unstated candidate. Because this diagnostic needs only labeled episodes and a frozen reader, it applies to any importance judgment. We evaluate six open-weight models from three families (7B to 32B) on 363 test episodes.

The Mesopotamia example is representative. The importance report ranks an implied useful concept above a stated distractor in only 38.3\% of comparisons (six-model mean), yet above an unstated distractor in 92.4\%; when it misses an implied useful concept, 95--99\% of stored concepts are passage words. This preference for stated candidates appears in all six models in the importance report on both pools and in the 1--10 importance rating on the statedness-balanced pool; on Qwen3-8B, both ConsistencyGate forms and A-MAC utility also favor stated candidates. Missing knowledge does not explain the bias, and rewording the prompt does not remove it: the report ranks the same implied concepts above unstated distractors, and on Qwen3-8B the statedness-cued wording reverses the preference toward unstated candidates rather than removing it. On the original pool, the model's logit-lens readout and output likelihood favor unstated candidates in every model. The Statedness Bias is thus a property of the importance judgment, whereas the knowledge needed to admit implied concepts is already in the model. Admission evaluation, conversational or not, should therefore control statedness. Self-Distilled Admission (SDA) trains the importance judgment on these signals, with a LoRA adapter \citep{hu2021lora} used only for admission; with two stored concepts, it raises six-model mean retention of implied useful concepts from 33.4\% to 63.8\%.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We identify the Statedness Bias in LLM memory admission: importance judgments favor candidates because the passage states them, although the model recognizes the implied concept.
\item We introduce a diagnostic that crosses the statedness of useful concepts with that of distractors, and Self-Distilled Admission (SDA), which trains the importance judgment on the model's own signals.
\item SDA raises the win rate of implied useful concepts against stated distractors from 38.3\% to 67.5\% and exceeds the importance report and the 1--10 importance rating in answer accuracy for all six models and three budgets on the statedness-balanced pool.
\end{itemize}
