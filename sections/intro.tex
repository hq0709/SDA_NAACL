Advances in large language models (LLMs) have enabled agents that reason, use tools, and carry out tasks over multiple steps. To support extended tasks and repeated interactions, agents need to retain observations, experiences, and decisions. External memory provides continuity beyond the model's immediate context, allowing agents to draw on past interactions when reasoning about new tasks and questions \citep{packer2023memgpt,mem0,amem2025}. Its value, however, depends on what the agent chooses to preserve. Under a limited storage budget, agents must select concepts from a supplied candidate pool before future questions are known.

Existing systems provide mechanisms for managing, consolidating, and organizing memories \citep{packer2023memgpt,mem0,amem2025}. To prioritize information, systems use LLM importance ratings, such as Generative Agents' 1--10 score \citep{park2023generative}, and admission criteria, such as ConsistencyGate and A-MAC \citep{consistencygate2026,amac2026}. These judgments can conflate two distinct properties: \emph{statedness}, whether a passage explicitly states a concept, and \emph{usefulness}, whether a future question needs it. Figure~\ref{fig:episode} illustrates the consequence. Yara examines cuneiform tablets beside a river that fed early agriculture, but the passage never names Mesopotamia. An importance report selects \texttt{silt} and \texttt{tablet}, leaving the frozen reader unable to answer which modern country she visited. Selecting \texttt{mesopotamia} and \texttt{tablet} instead enables it to answer Iraq. The useful concept requires a bridging inference from the passage to prior knowledge \citep{haviland1974whatsnew}. We call the preference for explicitly stated concepts \textbf{Statedness Bias}. Existing conversational benchmarks do not isolate this distinction \citep{maharana2024locomo,wu2025longmemeval}, leaving a central question unresolved: how can memory admission preserve useful implications when importance judgments favor explicit mention?

To address this problem, we propose \textbf{Self-Distilled Admission (SDA)}, which aligns memory selection with the model's ability to recognize useful concepts. The underlying principle is that memory should preserve the situation a passage describes, including supported inferences, consistent with how readers understand and remember text \citep{bransford1972sentence,kintsch1988construction}. Our approach builds on a mismatch within the model: it can recognize an implied useful concept while assigning that concept low importance. Signals from its internal representations and output likelihoods show a weaker statedness preference than its explicit importance judgments. SDA uses these signals to supervise the admission judgment, transferring the model's existing recognition into its memory decisions. The same principle applies to stated and implied concepts, allowing useful information to compete for storage without making explicit mention the basis of its importance.

Concretely, SDA trains an admission-only LoRA adapter using logit-lens readouts and output likelihoods \citep{hu2021lora}. Our diagnostic crosses the statedness of useful concepts and distractors. Within each model, all policies share the same candidates, budgets, and frozen reader. The bias is strong when implied useful concepts lose more comparisons against stated distractors than they win. Across six models, SDA improves useful-concept retention over the importance report on the statedness-balanced pool. At a two-concept budget, mean retention of implied concepts rises from 33.4\% to 63.8\%. For stated concepts, it rises from 45.3\% to 80.3\%. The Qwen3-8B adapter is also evaluated on admission episodes constructed from TwoHopFact, without training on that dataset. It achieves the highest answer accuracy among the evaluated non-reference policies at all three budgets. Together, these results show that SDA improves useful-concept retention across statedness conditions and downstream answer accuracy. Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We identify \textbf{Statedness Bias}: importance judgments favor explicitly stated concepts even when models recognize useful implications.
\item We introduce a statedness diagnostic that separates recognition from admission bias, and SDA, which trains admission using the model's own signals.
\item We show that SDA removes the strong form of Statedness Bias and exceeds the report and rating in all 18 model-budget comparisons on the statedness-balanced pool, with replication on public TwoHopFact.
\end{itemize}
